# Optical Overshoot

## Principle

Geometrically equal elements do not always appear visually equal. Differences in shape, area, curvature, and surrounding space can affect perceived size and alignment. Optical overshoot is the practice of making small visual adjustments so elements appear balanced to the human eye.

The goal is optical balance, not mathematical uniformity.

## When to Use

Apply this principle when designing or reviewing:
- Icons and logos
- Buttons containing circular or triangular symbols
- Geometric illustrations
- Typography and icon alignment
- Circular avatars and badges beside square elements
- UI components that need precise visual alignment

## How to Apply

- Compare how elements appear visually, not only their measured dimensions.
- Check circular, triangular, and pointed shapes against flat-edged shapes.
- Adjust size or position slightly when a shape appears smaller or misaligned despite having mathematically equal dimensions.
- Evaluate icons inside buttons and other fixed-size containers to ensure they feel centered.
- Use visual inspection at the actual rendered size rather than relying only on enlarged previews.
- Make adjustments in small increments and compare the result before settling on a change.

## Do

- Prioritize perceived balance when geometric alignment looks visually incorrect.
- Inspect shapes in context with their neighboring elements.
- Make subtle, purposeful adjustments.
- Check alignment across different sizes and display densities.
- Keep optical adjustments consistent across related components.

## Don't

- Automatically enlarge every circle by a fixed percentage.
- Assume every shape needs optical correction.
- Alter dimensions without checking the rendered result.
- Sacrifice consistent interaction targets or accessibility to achieve visual balance.
- Apply adjustments that look correct in isolation but create imbalance within the complete interface.

## Exceptions

Optical overshoot is not always necessary. Many icons, fonts, and component libraries already include optical corrections. The appropriate adjustment depends on the shape, typeface, size, surrounding elements, and visual context.

## Validation

- Do geometrically equal elements appear appropriately balanced?
- Do icons look centered within their containers?
- Are adjustments subtle and justified by visual perception?
- Do related elements still feel consistent?
- Does the result look balanced at its actual display size?

The goal is to achieve perceived visual balance where needed, not to apply arbitrary size adjustments to every interface.
